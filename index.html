European Media Freedom Act

Regulation (EU) 2024/1083 of the European Parliament and of the Council

The first comprehensive EU-wide legal framework establishing common safeguards for media freedom, editorial independence, and journalist protection across all Member States.

Overview

Key Pillars of the EMFA

The European Media Freedom Act establishes a comprehensive framework built on four foundational pillars to protect media freedom across the EU.

Editorial Independence

Binding safeguards against political and commercial interference in editorial decisions. Neither state bodies nor regulators may influence content.

Article 4 →

Source Protection

Comprehensive protection for journalists and their sources against surveillance, with strict limitations on spyware deployment.

Articles 4-5 →

Transparency

Mandatory disclosure of media ownership structures, state advertising allocations, and audience measurement methodologies.

Articles 6, 23-24 →

Platform Accountability

Special obligations for very large online platforms regarding media content moderation and dialogue requirements.

Article 17 →

Legal Analysis

Article-by-Article Deconstruction

Comprehensive breakdown of all 26 articles across 5 chapters, with legal analysis and practical implications.

Chapter I: General Provisions

Establishes the foundational scope, objectives, and definitions of the regulation. These provisions determine who is covered and set key terminology used throughout the Act.

Key Provisions
  • Establishes common framework for media services in the internal market
  • Applies to media service providers established in the Union
  • Covers providers established outside EU but targeting EU audiences
  • Sets rules for protection of natural persons using media services
Legal Analysis

Scope Determination: The regulation employs a dual-pronged jurisdictional approach. Primary jurisdiction is based on establishment within the Union. Secondary jurisdiction captures non-EU providers "directing activities" toward Union audiences—mirroring the approach in the DSA and GDPR.

Material Scope: Covers all "media services" as defined in Article 2, encompassing traditional broadcast, press, and digital media. Notably excludes purely private communications and user-generated content not edited by media service providers.

Practical Implications
✓

Ensures comprehensive coverage of all significant media actors

⚠

May create compliance challenges for smaller digital-first outlets

Related Recitals

Recitals 1-10 provide interpretive context

Cross-References
  • Art. 2 AVMSD (definitions)

Key Definitions
'media service'
A service as defined by Articles 56 and 57 TFEU, where the principal purpose is the provision to the general public of programmes or press publications for information, entertainment or education, under editorial responsibility.
'media service provider'
A natural or legal person whose professional activity is the provision of a media service and who has editorial responsibility for the choice of content.
'editorial responsibility'
Effective control over both the selection of content and its organization, whether in a linear schedule or a catalogue.
'editorial decision'
A decision taken regularly for the purpose of exercising editorial responsibility, linked to the day-to-day operation of the media service provider.
'public service media provider'
A media service provider entrusted with a public service remit, established by law or by administrative act.
'audience measurement'
Activity of collecting, interpreting or otherwise processing data about the number and characteristics of users of media services.
'state advertising'
Promotion or self-promotion activities undertaken by or on behalf of a public authority, including national, regional, or local governments.
Legal Analysis

Editorial Responsibility Test: Central to EMFA's scope. The requirement for "effective control" means aggregators and platforms generally fall outside—unless they exercise actual editorial judgment over content selection or presentation.

Media Service Definition: Intentionally broad to capture evolving media landscape. The "principal purpose" test excludes services where media content is ancillary (e.g., e-commerce sites with blogs).

Key Interpretation

The definition of "editorial responsibility" is crucial—it determines whether an entity qualifies for EMFA protections.

Key Provisions
  • Right to receive a plurality of media services
  • Right to access information about media ownership
  • Right to transparent information about editorial policies
  • Framework for protecting minors while ensuring access
Legal Analysis

Novel Rights Framework: Article 3 creates subjective rights for media recipients—a significant departure from traditional media regulation which focused primarily on provider obligations. These rights are directly enforceable before national courts.

Plurality Principle: The right to "plurality" implies a positive obligation on Member States to ensure diverse media ecosystems—going beyond merely preventing monopolies.

Chapter II: Rights and Duties of Media Service Providers and their Recipients

The substantive heart of the EMFA. Contains the critical protections for editorial independence, source protection, and safeguards against surveillance that make this regulation groundbreaking.

Paragraph Breakdown
Paragraph 1 - State Non-Interference
"Member States shall respect the actual editorial freedom of media service providers. Member States, including their national regulatory authorities or bodies, shall not interfere in or try to influence in any way, directly or indirectly, the editorial policies and decisions of media service providers."
Analysis: Creates an absolute prohibition on state interference. The phrase "actual editorial freedom" is significant—it targets de facto pressures, not merely formal restrictions. The inclusion of regulatory authorities within the prohibition addresses concerns about regulatory capture.
Paragraph 2 - Source Protection
"Member States shall ensure effective protection of journalistic sources and communications... This protection shall apply to the seizure and disclosure of journalistic sources and communications, searches of professional and private premises, as well as to surveillance measures."
Analysis: Extends protection beyond traditional source confidentiality to encompass the full spectrum of investigative methods. The explicit mention of "surveillance measures" signals the regulation's response to spyware scandals.
Paragraph 3 - Spyware Restrictions
"The deployment of spyware against media service providers, journalists, or their family members shall be prohibited, unless in justified cases on grounds of national security..."
Analysis: The "family members" extension addresses tactics used to pressure journalists indirectly. The national security exception requires case-by-case judicial authorization—no blanket permissions.
Enforcement Mechanisms
  • Right to be informed after surveillance has occurred
  • Right to judicial review of surveillance decisions
  • Mandatory notification to affected journalists
  • Compensation rights for unlawful surveillance
Implementation Note

Member States must establish or designate competent authorities for enforcement by August 2025.

Key Recitals
  • Recital 16 - Editorial freedom
  • Recital 17 - Source protection
  • Recital 18 - Spyware prohibition
Governance Requirements
  • Appointment Procedures: Directors must be selected through transparent, open, and non-discriminatory procedures based on objective criteria
  • Term Protection: Sufficient term length to ensure actual independence; dismissal only for good cause
  • Funding Transparency: Adequate and stable funding determined in advance through transparent processes
  • Judicial Review: Right to challenge dismissal decisions in court
Legal Analysis

Addressing Political Capture: These provisions directly respond to concerns about governmental influence over public broadcasters in several Member States. The requirement for "objective criteria" in appointments aims to prevent political patronage.

Funding Independence: The "advance determination" requirement prevents governments from using budget leverage for editorial influence—a common tactic in media capture scenarios.

Transparency Obligations

Ownership Disclosure

Beneficial owners, direct/indirect shareholders with significant holdings

State advertising received, public funding sources

📝
Editorial Information

Contact details, editorial policies, complaint mechanisms

Information must be easily accessible and regularly updated

Internal Safeguards

Media service providers must ensure appropriate measures exist to guarantee independence of individual editorial decisions. This includes:

  • Internal procedures protecting editorial staff from owner/shareholder interference
  • Editorial charters or equivalent mechanisms
  • Whistleblower protection for journalists reporting interference
User Control Over Recommendations

Recipients have the right to easily change default settings of devices or interfaces that affect their access to media services. Applies particularly to:

  • Smart TVs and connected devices
  • Operating system default media apps
  • Algorithmic recommendation systems

Application Timeline: This provision applies 36 months after entry into force (May 2027), giving device manufacturers time to implement necessary changes.

Chapter III: European Board for Media Services

Establishes the institutional framework for EU-level coordination, replacing ERGA with an enhanced European Board for Media Services with greater powers and responsibilities.

Institutional Foundation

The European Board for Media Services replaces the European Regulators Group for Audiovisual Media Services (ERGA). Key changes:

  • Enhanced legal status and powers
  • Permanent secretariat support from Commission
  • Expanded mandate beyond audiovisual to all media services
  • Formal role in cross-border enforcement
Composition
  • One representative per Member State from national regulatory authority
  • Chair and Vice-Chair elected from members (1-year term, renewable once)
  • Commission representative (observer, no vote)
Independence Requirements

Board members must be independent and act in the public interest. They may not seek or take instructions from governments or other bodies. Explicit prohibition on conflict of interest.

Core Functions
Advisory Role (Art. 11)

Provide opinions to Commission on EMFA implementation, media market issues, and cross-border matters

Coordination (Art. 12)

Coordinate national regulatory actions on cross-border services and market concentrations

Guidelines (Art. 13)

Develop guidelines on regulatory cooperation, audience measurement, state advertising

Dispute Resolution (Art. 14)

Mediate regulatory disagreements between Member States

Chapter IV: Framework for an Open Media Market

Addresses market-related provisions including platform obligations, media concentrations, audience measurement, and state advertising transparency.

Platform Obligations
1. Priority Complaint Handling

VLOPs must process complaints from media service providers "with priority and without undue delay." This creates a two-tier system differentiating professional media from user-generated content.

2. Pre-Suspension Notice

Before restricting or suspending media content, platforms must provide a statement of reasons to the affected media service provider, allowing response before action (except in exceptional circumstances).

3. Meaningful Dialogue Requirement

Where media service providers experience repeated restrictions, platforms must engage in "meaningful and effective dialogue... in good faith" to find amicable solutions.

4. Annual Transparency Reporting

Platforms must publish annual reports on restrictions applied to media content, number of dialogues initiated, and outcomes.

Legal Analysis

DSA Interface: Article 17 creates a lex specialis for media content within the DSA framework. Where EMFA provides specific rules, they prevail over general DSA provisions. However, DSA obligations (like illegal content removal) remain applicable.

Self-Identification Mechanism: Media service providers must be able to self-identify to platforms. Platforms may verify this claim but cannot impose unreasonable requirements.

Affected Platforms

VLOPs as defined under DSA Article 33 (>45 million monthly active users in EU)

  • Facebook/Meta
  • YouTube
  • TikTok
  • X (Twitter)
  • LinkedIn
  • And others designated by Commission
National Assessment Requirements

Member States must establish legislative, regulatory, or administrative measures requiring assessment of media market concentrations that could significantly impact media pluralism and editorial independence.

Assessment Criteria
  • Impact on media pluralism
  • Safeguards for editorial independence
  • Economic sustainability considerations
  • Measures to guarantee content diversity
Board's Role

The Board can issue opinions on concentrations with cross-border dimension or affecting internal market functioning. National authorities must give "utmost account" to these opinions.

Database Requirements

Member States must establish or designate national databases containing comprehensive information about media service providers within their jurisdiction, including:

  • Legal name and contact details
  • Beneficial ownership structure
  • Shareholders with significant holdings
  • State funding and advertising received

Information must be publicly accessible and regularly updated.

Transparency Requirements

Audience measurement providers must ensure their methodologies are:

  • Transparent and verifiable
  • Non-discriminatory
  • Consistent with industry standards
Proprietary Measurement Systems

Where VLOPs maintain proprietary audience measurement systems, they must provide transparent information about methodologies to media service providers upon request.

Core Principles
⚖️
Non-Discrimination

State advertising must be allocated according to transparent, objective, proportionate, and non-discriminatory criteria

📊
Public Reporting

Annual publication of state advertising expenditure per media service provider

🎯
Objective Criteria

Allocation decisions based on reach, audience profile, and cost-effectiveness—not editorial stance

Legal Analysis

Anti-Capture Mechanism: State advertising has been used as a tool for media capture in several Member States. By requiring transparent, criteria-based allocation, EMFA limits governments' ability to reward favorable coverage or punish critical reporting.

Chapter V: Final Provisions

Contains procedural provisions regarding monitoring, evaluation, amendments to existing legislation, and entry into force.

Review Mechanisms
  • Commission to evaluate EMFA implementation 4 years after application
  • Assessment of effects on media freedom and pluralism
  • Review of Board's functioning and effectiveness
  • Consideration of whether amendments are necessary

FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

The Audiovisual Media Services Directive (AVMSD) focuses on audiovisual content (TV, video-on-demand) and is a directive requiring national implementation. EMFA is a regulation (directly applicable) with broader scope covering all media services including press. EMFA specifically targets editorial independence, source protection, and platform accountability—areas not addressed by AVMSD. EMFA also amends AVMSD to enhance the European Board for Media Services (formerly ERGA).

Yes, but in a specific way. Very Large Online Platforms (VLOPs) under the DSA are subject to Article 17 obligations regarding media content on their platforms. However, platforms themselves are generally not "media service providers" under EMFA because they don't exercise editorial responsibility over content. The key obligations for VLOPs include priority complaint handling for media providers, pre-suspension notice requirements, and meaningful dialogue obligations.

EMFA significantly restricts but does not absolutely prohibit surveillance. Spyware deployment against journalists is prohibited unless: (1) it's justified on grounds of national security in individual cases, (2) authorized by a judicial authority, and (3) investigating serious crimes punishable by custodial sentences. Even when permitted, journalists have the right to be informed after surveillance ends and can challenge the surveillance in court.

EMFA enforcement operates at multiple levels: (1) National regulatory authorities monitor compliance and can take enforcement action; (2) The European Board for Media Services coordinates cross-border enforcement and can issue opinions; (3) Individuals and media providers can bring claims directly before national courts as EMFA creates directly enforceable rights; (4) The European Commission can initiate infringement proceedings against non-compliant Member States.

EMFA requires Member States to establish measures for assessing media market concentrations that could impact pluralism. It mandates national databases with ownership information and requires media providers to disclose beneficial ownership. However, EMFA doesn't set specific ownership limits—these remain within Member State competence. The Board can issue opinions on concentrations with cross-border implications, which national authorities must give "utmost account."
